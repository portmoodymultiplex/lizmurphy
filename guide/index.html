Chapter two

Is Mexico right for you?

Before you look at a single listing, get clear on what kind of Mexico life you actually want. There are two common paths.

The snowbird

You keep your BC home and spend the winter months in Mexico. Lower commitment, two homes to look after.

The full move

You sell in BC and make Mexico home base. More freedom and usually more money in your pocket, but a bigger leap.

7Is Mexico right for you?

Quick self-check

Answer these honestly. There are no wrong answers, but they'll tell you which path fits.

  1. How many months a year do you actually want to be in Mexico?
  2. Do you need to stay close to kids, grandkids or aging parents in BC?
  3. Would you rather have your equity working for you, or keep a home to come back to?
  4. Are you comfortable managing a property from a distance, or would you hire help?
  5. How important is being near family doctors and specialists you already know?
  6. Have you spent at least a few weeks in Bucerías in both high season and low season?

Rent before you buy

If you haven't spent a full season in Bucerías, rent first. A winter in town will tell you more than any guide, including this one. You'll learn which streets are quiet, which are party central on a Saturday, and whether you can handle the heat in June.

Chapter three

How Canadians legally own property on the coast

Yes, Canadians can own property in Bucerías. It just works a little differently than at home.

The restricted zone

Mexico's constitution restricts foreigners from holding direct title to land within 50 km of the coast or 100 km of a border. Bucerías, being right on the beach, is inside that zone.

The fideicomiso (bank trust)

The solution is a fideicomiso, a trust held by a Mexican bank on your behalf. The bank holds title, but you are the beneficiary with full rights to use, rent, renovate, sell, or leave the property to your heirs. It's a well-established system that foreign buyers have used for decades.

Ejido land: walk away

Ejido land is communal agricultural land. Unless it has been fully and properly converted to private property, a foreigner can't securely own it, no matter what the seller says or how good the price looks. If you hear the word "ejido," ask a lot of questions, and be prepared to walk away.

Chapter four

The buying process, step by step

From accepted offer to keys in hand typically takes about 60 days (per Bienvenidos Real Estate). Here's how it usually goes.

  1. Find the property with a reputable local agent.

  2. Make an offer. Once accepted, you sign a promise to purchase (promesa de compraventa).

  3. Pay your deposit, typically 10% of the price, into a third-party escrow account. Never to the seller or an agent directly.

  4. The notario público reviews title, permits and taxes, and the bank sets up your fideicomiso.

  5. The property is appraised and paperwork is finalized.

  6. You sign the deed in front of the notario, the balance is released from escrow, and you get your keys.

11The buying process

The notario is not a Canadian notary

In Mexico, the notario público is a lawyer appointed by the state and one of the most important people in your purchase. They confirm clean title, calculate and collect taxes, and register the sale. Your agent will work with a notario they trust, but you can also choose your own.

Rule of thumb: if anyone asks you to wire money directly to them instead of to escrow, stop and call me.

Chapter six

Living there: residency, healthcare and banking

Your options for staying

  • Visitor: Canadians can enter as visitors, but the length of stay is decided at the border and isn't guaranteed. Fine for a season, not a plan for living there.
  • Temporary residency: for stays longer than six months, renewable for up to four years. You apply at a Mexican consulate in Canada (Vancouver has one) before you go, then finish the process in Mexico.
  • Permanent residency: available after four years as a temporary resident, or directly if you meet higher financial requirements.
16Living there

The money test

To qualify for temporary residency, you need to show either steady monthly income or a healthy savings balance. In 2026, that works out to roughly US$4,300 to $4,500 per month in income over the last six months, or roughly US$73,000 to $75,000 in average savings over the last year. The thresholds change every year, so check with the consulate before you apply.

Healthcare

Puerto Vallarta, about 30 minutes away, has well-regarded private hospitals, and many expats carry private health insurance. Remember that BC MSP has residency rules: if you're away too many months of the year, you can lose your coverage. Check with Health Insurance BC before you plan a long stay.

Banking and paperwork

Once you're a resident, you'll get a CURP (Mexico's personal ID number), which makes opening a local bank account, setting up utilities, and getting a phone plan much easier.

Chapter seven

What to do with your BC home

For most Canadians, the BC home is the biggest asset they own. What you do with it shapes everything else about your move: your budget in Mexico, your taxes, and whether you have a way back.

What your equity could look like

Here in New Westminster, a typical 2-bedroom, 2-bathroom condo sells for roughly $600,000 to $700,000, and a townhome for roughly $1 million to $1.2 million.

Example scenario (illustrative, not a real client): a couple owns a mortgage-free townhome in New West worth about $1.1 million. They sell and buy a 2-bedroom condo in Bucerías for around US$350,000, which is about C$480,000 to C$500,000 depending on the exchange rate. After costs on both ends, that can leave well over half a million dollars for retirement, travel, and the occasional margarita.

Same couple, different choice: they keep the townhome and snowbird. That means a second property to carry and a different set of rules to follow.

19Your BC home

Questions to ask your accountant before you decide

  • If I sell now, is my whole gain covered by the principal residence exemption?
  • What happens, tax-wise, if I become a non-resident of Canada?
  • If I sell after I've moved, what is a clearance certificate and how long will it hold up my money?
  • If I turn my home into a rental, what are the tax consequences, and are there elections I should make?
  • How is rent taxed if I'm living in Mexico?
  • Will the Speculation and Vacancy Tax apply to my situation?

Sell first or buy first?

  • Sell first: you know exactly what you have to spend, and there's no carrying two properties. You may need a short-term rental in between.
  • Buy first: you lock in the right place in Bucerías, but you may carry two properties for a while.
  • The middle path: sell in BC, rent in Bucerías for a season, then buy once you know which street you love.

Chapter eight

Renting out your Mexico property

Many owners rent their place out when they're not using it, which can cover the carrying costs and then some.

Monthly rent, 2 bed / 2 bath condoAbout $2,000 to $3,000 per month, plus utilities (USD, per Bienvenidos Real Estate)
Property management feeAbout 20% of rental income

What to know

  • High season (roughly November to April) is when the snowbirds arrive and demand peaks. Summer is hot, humid and much quieter.
  • A good property manager handles bookings, cleaning, maintenance and guest issues. From 3,000 km away, that's worth the fee. Bienvenidos Real Estate offers rentals and property management in-house and manages about 25 properties in the area.
  • Rental income earned in Mexico is taxable in Mexico, and you may also need to report it in Canada. Get cross-border tax advice before your first guest arrives.

Chapter nine

Seven mistakes Canadians make

  • Buying ejido land. If it isn't properly converted to private property, you don't really own it.

  • Wiring money without escrow. Deposits go to a third-party escrow company. Always.

  • Skipping title insurance. It's inexpensive peace of mind on the biggest purchase you'll make there.

  • Working with an unvetted agent. Real estate isn't regulated in Mexico the way it is in BC. Ask whether your agent is a member of AMPI, Mexico's national real estate association, and how long they've been working locally.

  • Buying pre-construction from an unproven developer. Check the developer's track record and completed projects before you hand over a deposit.

  • Buying sight unseen. Photos and video tours are great. Standing on the balcony at 3 p.m. in May is better.

  • Forgetting about the BC side. Taxes, timing and what happens to your BC home matter just as much as the Mexico purchase.
