Can Canadians own beachfront property in Mexico? How the fideicomiso works.

Short answer: yes. Longer answer: it works differently than at home, and the difference matters.

The Second Coast

By Liz Murphy, REALTOR® · Zada Group · 6 min read

It's the first question almost every Canadian asks me about Mexico, usually a little nervously: can I actually own a place on the beach down there?

You can. Thousands of Canadians already do, and the system they use has been around for decades. I lived in Bucerías for seven years and sold real estate there with my dad, so I've walked a lot of people through it. It just isn't the same as buying a condo in Vancouver, and the people who get into trouble are usually the ones who assumed it was.

1. The restricted zone

Mexico's constitution doesn't allow foreigners to hold direct title to land within 50 kilometres of the coast or 100 kilometres of an international border. That band is called the restricted zone.

Bucerías sits right on the beach, so it's inside the zone. So is everything else most Canadians picture when they picture Mexico: La Cruz de Huanacaxtle, Punta de Mita, Sayulita, San Pancho, Nuevo Vallarta, Puerto Vallarta itself. If it has an ocean view, assume you're in the restricted zone.

Inland cities like Guadalajara or San Miguel de Allende sit outside it, and foreigners can hold title there directly. But that's not usually why people call me.

2. What a fideicomiso actually is

The workaround is a fideicomiso, a trust held by a Mexican bank on your behalf. The bank holds title. You are the beneficiary, with full rights to use the property, rent it out, renovate it, sell it, or leave it to your heirs.

People hear "the bank holds title" and get uneasy, which is fair. It helps to understand what the bank is and isn't doing. The bank is a trustee, not an owner. It can't sell your condo, borrow against it, or move into it. Its job is to hold the title on your instruction, and you pay it an annual fee to do that. When you sell, the fideicomiso is either transferred to your buyer or a new one is set up for them.

A few things that are useful to know going in:

  • It runs for 50 years and is renewable. This isn't a lease that expires on you.
  • You name your beneficiaries. Who inherits the property is set out in the trust itself, which can make things simpler for your family later. Talk to the notario about how this lines up with your Canadian will.
  • You can rent it. Plenty of owners cover their carrying costs with rental income during high season, roughly November to April.

3. What it costs

These are ballpark figures from Bienvenidos Real Estate in Bucerías, in US dollars, as of November 2026:

  • Fideicomiso setup: about $1,000
  • Annual bank fee: about $566 a year
  • Closing costs, all in: about 4% to 6% of the purchase price
  • Escrow fee (buyer paid): about $750
  • Deposit: typically 10% of the price, held in escrow

For context, a two-bedroom, two-bathroom condo in Bucerías currently starts around US$350,000. On that, closing costs land somewhere around $14,000 to $21,000. In Mexico the buyer pays closing costs, which surprises people who are used to the Canadian split. Your agent and notario will give you exact numbers for the specific property before you commit.

4. The notario is not a Canadian notary

This is the one I wish every buyer understood before they flew down. In Mexico, the notario público is a lawyer appointed by the state, and one of the most important people in your purchase. They confirm the title is clean, calculate and collect the taxes, and register the sale. A Canadian notary witnesses signatures. A Mexican notario makes sure what you're buying is actually what you think you're buying.

Your agent will work with a notario they trust, but you're allowed to choose your own.

If anyone asks you to wire money directly to them instead of to escrow, stop and call me.

5. Ejido land: walk away

Ejido land is communal agricultural land. Unless it has been fully and properly converted to private property, a foreigner can't securely own it, no matter what the seller says or how good the price looks.

Ejido deals tend to show up as the bargain lot with the incredible view, or the "great opportunity" a friend of a friend heard about. If you hear the word ejido, ask a lot of questions, and be prepared to walk away. A good notario will catch it. A bad deal will try to keep the notario out of it.

6. From offer to keys

From an accepted offer to keys in hand typically takes about 60 days. It usually goes like this:

  1. Find the property with a reputable local agent.
  2. Make an offer. Once it's accepted, you sign a promise to purchase (promesa de compraventa).
  3. Pay your deposit, typically 10%, into a third-party escrow account. Never to the seller or an agent directly.
  4. The notario reviews title, permits and taxes, and the bank sets up your fideicomiso.
  5. The property is appraised and the paperwork is finalized.
  6. You sign the deed in front of the notario, the balance is released from escrow, and you get your keys.

Real estate isn't regulated in Mexico the way it is in BC, which is exactly why the people you choose matter. Ask whether your agent is a member of AMPI, Mexico's national real estate association, and how long they've worked locally. Add title insurance. It's inexpensive peace of mind on the biggest purchase you'll make there.

7. Frequently asked questions

Is a fideicomiso safe?

It's a well-established system that foreign buyers have used for decades, and the bank acts as trustee on your instruction. The risks in Mexican real estate usually come from somewhere else: ejido land, money wired outside of escrow, unproven pre-construction developers, or skipping the notario's due diligence.

Can I leave my Mexico property to my kids?

Yes. You name beneficiaries within the fideicomiso. It's worth having the notario and your estate lawyer in Canada look at it together so the two plans don't contradict each other.

Do I need to be a resident of Mexico to buy?

No. You can buy as a visitor. Residency matters for how long you can stay, not whether you can own. If you plan to live there more than six months a year, look at temporary residency, which you apply for at a Mexican consulate in Canada before you go.

Should I rent before I buy?

If you haven't spent a full season in Bucerías, yes. A winter in town will tell you which streets are quiet, which are loud on a Saturday night, and whether you can handle the heat in June.
